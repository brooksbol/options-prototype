# Commit to the process—but not blindly

**Adversarial evidence review · 36-source ledger**

What the evidence actually says about choosing a decision program in advance, adhering under pressure, allowing principled override, and changing the program without rewriting history.

**Research scope:** 9 domains — prediction, finance, forecasting, medicine, aviation, military doctrine, organizations, automation, control.  
**No direct verdict:** No study found isolates adherence from the quality of the chosen program.  
**Reviewed:** September 25, 2026.

## Executive verdict

> **A narrower claim survives.** Good, pre-specified procedures often beat unstructured contemporaneous judgment. Outcome-triggered revision is hazardous, and stable policies improve attribution. But adherence is not an independent virtue: rigid execution of a misspecified program can magnify harm.

## 01 — Executive answer

The general hypothesis—“select a coherent decision program, stick to it, and decisions improve”—is **not directly established**. The literature supports structure over improvisation, but it does not isolate whether value comes from the program’s quality, from the act of adherence, or from the pause and deliberation involved in choosing it.

### What survives

Mechanical prediction, checklists, structured forecasting, and restraint under noisy feedback often outperform unstructured judgment.

### Why it may work

Lower judgment noise, fewer omission errors, less overtrading, less outcome-biased revision, and a stable baseline for learning.

### What fails

Rigid programs break under structural change, model misspecification, rare facts outside the inputs, and poorly calibrated stop rules.

### The missing identification

No study found holds program quality fixed while experimentally varying adherence. The core causal claim remains untested.

> The responsible rule is not “never deviate.” It is: select deliberately, execute faithfully, define exceptions in advance, document departures, and revise through a process that treats outcomes as evidence—not verdicts.

**Close proxy — Grove et al.** Grove et al.’s 136-study meta-analysis found mechanical combination about 10% more accurate than clinical judgment; formulas were substantially better in 33–47% of studies, clinicians in 6–16%. That is a strong result for structured prediction, but not a clean test of commitment.

**Direct mechanism — Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey.** Five incentivized experiments found that people abandoned a superior algorithm after watching it make mistakes. A later study found that allowing slight modification increased both adoption and performance—evidence for bounded discretion, not total rigidity.

## 02 — Keep four dimensions separate

A lucky outcome does not validate a decision; an adverse outcome does not invalidate it. The evidence demands four distinct objects of evaluation.

1. **Program quality** — Was the procedure well-specified and reasonable ex ante for the environment?
2. **Adherence** — Did the operator execute the procedure as specified—or depart?
3. **Execution quality** — Was the chosen action carried out competently and with fidelity?
4. **Outcome** — What happened, including noise and factors no one controlled?

**Outcome bias is measured, not hypothetical.** Baron & Hershey (1988) showed that people rated identical decisions differently based only on outcomes. In high-noise domains, revising a rule after one painful result is therefore not “learning”; it can be fitting noise.

## 03 — Evidence map by domain

Labels describe proximity to the causal claim, not study quality.

### Judgment & prediction — close proxy; leans structured

- **Grove et al. (2000):** 136 studies across medical, psychiatric, academic, occupational, criminal-justice and business prediction. Mechanical judgment was ~10% more accurate on average. Rules were human-built; the outcome was prediction accuracy, not downstream decisions.
- **Dawes, Faust & Meehl (1989):** limited research on the “broken-leg” exception found greater accuracy when clinicians relied uniformly on actuarial methods. The authors themselves called the evidence limited.
- **Noise audits:** identical cases produced 55% median premium variation among underwriters and 41% valuation variation among analysts—documenting the inconsistency that structure may reduce, not testing the remedy.

### Behavioral finance & trading — close proxy; leans structured

- **Barber & Odean (2000):** 66,465 US brokerage households, 1991–1996. Highest-turnover quintile earned 11.4% annually versus 18.5% for the lowest; market return was 17.9%. Observational, bull-market data; period trading costs matter.
- **Odean (1999):** in 10,000 accounts, purchased stocks underperformed sold stocks by 23 basis points per month over the next year, even after excluding liquidity, tax and rebalancing trades.
- The replicated disposition effect shows traders sell winners and ride losers. But the better observed alternative was often *inaction*, not an active precommitted program.

### Investment management — close proxy; mixed

- **Harvey et al. (2017):** hedge funds, 1996–2014. Systematic and discretionary performance was similar after adjusting for volatility and factor exposures.
- **Preqin industry data:** systematic funds lost 0.03% in 2008 versus 18.65% for discretionary; three-year Sharpes reported at 1.54 versus 0.80. This is industry research, not a randomized adherence test.
- **Dyer, Guest & Yu (2021):** CRSP mutual funds, 2003–2020. Quant funds underperformed discretionary funds by 2.73% a year following three accounting-standard changes—the cleanest evidence here that unmodeled structural breaks favor discretion.

### Forecasting — close proxy; mixed

- **Good Judgment Project:** 2011–2014; 2,500+ forecasters, 100,000+ predictions, 500 questions. Training, teams, aggregation, base rates and archived probabilities delivered ~35% Brier improvement and beat expert and market benchmarks.
- The best forecasters also updated *more frequently*. The stable commitment was to disciplined updating, not to an initial answer.
- Forecasts were short-horizon, resolvable, scored, and had no action consequences—far friendlier to learning than many real decisions.

### Medicine — close proxy; leans structured

- **Haynes et al. (2009):** 8 hospitals; 3,733 pre vs 3,955 post. Complications fell 11.0%→7.0%; inpatient deaths 1.5%→0.8%. Pre-post design leaves secular and Hawthorne confounds.
- **Haugen et al.:** stepped-wedge cluster RCT at 2 hospitals, 2009–2010; 2,212 control vs 2,263 checklist procedures. Complications fell 19.9%→11.5%; overall mortality change was not significant.
- Transfer is limited: surgical checklists reduce omissions of known-correct steps. They do not identify the correct action under deep uncertainty.

### Aviation & high reliability — doctrine / distant analogy; mixed

- CRM and FAA doctrine treat SOP compliance as positive behavior and procedures as a shared mental model.
- Wagener & Ison reviewed 113 final-approach accidents/incidents from 2002–2012; 57 had a CRM-related causal factor, yet found no significant relationship between management practices and CRM causes.
- Accident evidence is confounded: procedural deviation may cause an emergency—or respond to one.

### Military command — doctrine / distant analogy; leans adaptive

- **Mission command / Auftragstaktik:** commander precommits to intent; subordinates choose the method. It is a counter-model to complete procedural commitment.
- Historical and contemporary comparisons are suggestive but uncontrolled. The transfer claim is about organizational design, not individual decision accuracy.

### Organizations & commitment — theory / contested proxy; mixed

- **Kydland & Prescott (1977):** commitment can beat discretion when other agents form expectations; this is a game-theoretic mechanism, not evidence about a lone operator.
- **Escalation of commitment:** Staw and Brockner describe self-justifying reinvestment in failing courses. Białek et al. (2021), N=365, failed to replicate the flagship paradigm.

### Automation & control — close proxy; leans adaptive

- Automation bias produces omission and commission errors in novices and experts; training and teams do not eliminate it.
- Parasuraman et al. found failure detection of 75–82% with variable-reliability automation versus 33% with constantly reliable automation. Reliable systems can degrade vigilance.
- Forced reflection, structured override, and “show your work” workflows are the best-supported mitigators—not blind obedience.

## 04 — The strongest evidence for structure

1. **Mechanical prediction beats clinical judgment.** Grove et al.’s 136-study meta-analysis is the broadest and most consistent finding. It supports pre-specified data combination, not adherence as a free-standing cause.
2. **Uniform reliance beats ad hoc override.** The narrow broken-leg literature is directly relevant: allowing clinicians discretion to override actuarial output reduced overall accuracy. The evidence base was explicitly described as limited.
3. **Checklists reduce known omission failures.** The Haugen stepped-wedge RCT found an 8.4-point absolute reduction in complications. This is strong for execution of known-correct steps and weak for uncertain strategy selection.
4. **People abandon superior rules after visible errors.** Algorithm-aversion experiments identify the exact reactive-departure mechanism precommitment is meant to restrain.
5. **Noisy outcomes invite invalid revision.** Outcome bias, overtrading, the disposition effect, and multiple-testing corrections all point the same way: frequent intervention can be confidently wrong.

### Mechanisms: evidence versus plausibility

| Candidate mechanism | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Suppress emotional intervention | Algorithm aversion; disposition effect; outcome bias | Supported as a failure mode, but not as proof that binding commitment is the best cure. |
| Control action frequency | Barber & Odean turnover results | Moderate proxy evidence; transaction costs and selection confound causality. |
| Reduce omission & cognitive load | Surgical checklists; CRM practice | Supported in known-procedure domains. |
| Maintain exposure to an edge | No direct evidence found | Plausible, conditional on the edge being real and persistent. |
| Make observations comparable | Experimental-design logic; archived forecasts | Strong analytical support. |
| Improve causal learning | Bandit-policy comparison theory | Strong formal logic; no human program trial found. |
| Prevent escalation / rescue | Classic commitment studies, but a large failed replication | Contested. |

## 05 — Discretion wins when the rule is missing the world

A principled override is not merely an exception with a good story afterward. It is a departure whose trigger, scope, and record are constrained before the pressure arrives.

### Evidence for bounded override

- Slight algorithm modification increased both adoption and performance (Dietvorst et al., 2018).
- Aviation go-around criteria define objective deviations before approach.
- Forced reflection and “show your work” mitigate automation bias.
- Mission command commits to intent while delegating method.

### Evidence against free override

- Clinician discretion over actuarial rules reduced overall accuracy in limited tests.
- People abandon better algorithms after merely seeing them err.
- Discomfort and recent losses distort decisions even when information is unchanged.
- Rare “broken-leg” facts exist, but humans overclaim them.

### Principled override flow

**New evidence?** Fact unavailable to the program, a violated criterion, or a changed constraint.

→ **Predefined authority?** Trigger, allowed scope, and responsible operator are explicit.

→ **Document & review.** Record what changed, what was overridden, and whether the program needs revision.

**Principled override:** Triggered by new decision-relevant evidence; bounded ex ante; rationale recorded; preserves the original recommendation for later comparison.

**Reactive departure:** Same known situation, newly uncomfortable; trigger inferred after the fact; no limit; outcome used to rewrite the prior decision.

**Evidence gap:** no study found directly compares these two override types as predictors of success. The distinction is operationally clean and theoretically defensible, but not empirically validated as a classification rule.

## 06 — Revision is not departure

Execution applies the current program. Departure changes today’s action. Revision changes tomorrow’s program. Conflating them makes outcome-driven rationalization almost impossible to detect.

- **Execute** — Apply the existing rule to the current state.
- **Depart** — Override one recommendation without changing the rule.
- **Review** — Assess accumulated evidence at a pre-specified pause point.
- **Revise** — Version the program prospectively; never rewrite what was previously in force.

**Statistical warning.** Bailey & López de Prado’s Deflated Sharpe Ratio adjusts for the fact that the best result rises with the number of trials even when true skill is zero. Probability of Backtest Overfitting asks how often the in-sample winner fails out of sample; above 0.5, the selection process itself is overfitting. Every retrospective program tweak is another trial.

**Exception-policy evidence is mixed.** An ESMA study of roughly 10,000 instruments during April–December 2016 found circuit breakers reduced sudden volatility without harming price discovery, at the cost of wider spreads. China’s 5%/7% breakers in January 2016 amplified panic and were suspended within days. The lesson is not “always use stops”; it is that stop rules are themselves programs whose calibration must be validated.

**No evidence-based cadence emerged.** The literature found offers statistical guardrails—count trials, correct for selection, validate out of sample—but no validated rule saying “review quarterly,” “wait N observations,” or “change after X losses.” Those popular prescriptions remain unsupported.

## 07 — Stable policies buy information

Even when stability does not improve the next outcome, it can improve what the sequence of outcomes teaches.

Meng, Chen & Gao (2026) formalize the difficulty of comparing adaptive policies: each run produces one dependent trajectory, reliable inference needs many independent restarts, and naïve estimator variance can grow with horizon. In plain language, if the policy keeps changing, “the treatment” is not stable enough to evaluate.

Tetlock’s archived, timestamped forecasts serve the same purpose: they prevent later memory from polishing what was actually believed. A stable baseline plus a deviation log allows the analyst to ask whether departures helped. Without the baseline, program effects and operator effects are entangled.

> **Stable adherence is best understood as an attribution technology—not proof that the program is good.**

This claim is analytically strong and empirically thin. No study found demonstrates that people operating under stable decision programs actually learn faster or attribute causes better. The gambling precommitment study is a warning: reduced risk-taking appeared to come from the pre-choice deliberative pause, not from the restriction itself.

## 08 — A vocabulary that survives the literature

- **Program** — A decision procedure selected in advance; may include rules for updating and exceptions.
- **Adherence** — Executing the current program as specified.
- **Departure** — A momentary deviation that leaves the program itself unchanged.
- **Revision** — A deliberate, prospective change to the program.
- **Noise** — Unwanted variation in judgments of the same problem.
- **Resulting** — Judging decision quality from the realized outcome.
- **Broken-leg case** — A rare, decisive fact omitted from a rule’s inputs.
- **Principled override** — Bounded, criteria-triggered, documented departure.
- **Reactive departure** — Deviation triggered by pain, discomfort, recent outcomes, or a visible error without new information.
- **Pause point** — A pre-specified moment for verification or review.
- **Trial-counting** — Treating every tested revision as a statistical trial, including failed or hidden ones.
- **Intent / method** — Mission-command split: commit to desired end state, delegate how to reach it.
- **Ignorance** — The correct procedure is not known; the remedy is learning or research.
- **Ineptitude** — The correct procedure is known but not executed; the remedy may be adherence design.

## 09 — The strongest case against the premise

> **The hypothesis may mistake the container for the contents.**

### Good programs—not adherence—may explain every success

Mechanical prediction wins because the formula captures valid relationships. Surgical checklists work because their steps are correct. No study holds program quality constant and varies commitment. Strip away content quality and the independent contribution of adherence may be zero.

### Adherence can industrialize error

Quantitative funds underperformed after accounting-standard changes; China’s breakers amplified panic; automation bias survives expertise and training. A misspecified program, executed faithfully and at scale, can be more dangerous than slower human judgment.

### Nonstationary environments reward disciplined adaptation

The strongest forecasting program trained people to update more often. Mission command fixes intent and releases method. The relevant alternative to rigid commitment is not chaotic improvisation; it is structured adaptation. Against that alternative, direct evidence for adherence is absent.

### Deliberation may be the active ingredient

In the gambling study, precommitment trials reduced risk-taking, but restricting the available options added no benefit within those trials. The pause—not the binding—may have done the work.

### Metrics can corrupt judgment

Monitoring adherence can encourage compliance when a legitimate exception exists, while highly reliable automation erodes vigilance. A system that rewards “never deviated” risks hiding the exact expertise it needs during failure.

**Adversarial verdict:** the evidence supports good procedures, structured updating, bounded override, and restraint when feedback is noisy. “Adherence” as an independent virtue is unproven; as a universal prescription, it is unsafe.

## 10 — Major unresolved questions

1. Can adherence be separated causally from program quality?
2. When does skilled override of a good rule help rather than hurt?
3. How much of precommitment’s value is simply the deliberative pause?
4. Which kinds of structural change reliably favor discretion?
5. What review cadence and evidentiary threshold should govern revision?
6. Does stable adherence improve human learning in practice, not merely statistical identifiability?
7. Does measuring adherence create perverse incentives to obey bad recommendations?
8. When does tacit expert information add signal that the program cannot represent?
9. How far can known-procedure evidence from medicine and aviation transfer to open-ended uncertainty?

## 11 — The experiment the field still needs

To test adherence itself, hold the program fixed and randomize the freedom to depart.

1. **Same program, different adherence regime.** Randomize participants, desks, or teams to binding/costly departure versus free override, across many repeated consequential decisions.
2. **Cross program quality.** Use a factorial design: strong versus deliberately degraded program × bound versus free adherence. The crucial prediction is an interaction—commitment helps with good rules and hurts with bad ones.
3. **Separate deliberation from restriction.** Add a mandatory pre-decision pause with unrestricted override, isolating whether reflection rather than binding is causal.
4. **Measure four dimensions independently.** Score ex-ante program quality, observed adherence, execution fidelity, and outcomes. Preserve timestamped recommendation and departure logs.
5. **Vary the environment.** Include stationary periods and injected structural breaks to locate the boundary where discretion becomes valuable.

### What can responsibly be claimed?

Pre-specified, evidence-based procedures often outperform unstructured contemporaneous judgment. Restraint is valuable when feedback is noisy, and stable policies make learning more credible. But the evidence does not show that sticking to a chosen program is beneficial independently of whether that program is good.

### What evidence is still required?

A repeated-decision randomized study that gives different groups the same program but different override freedom; independently measures program quality, adherence, execution, and outcome; separates deliberation from binding; and spans stable and disrupted environments. No study in this review meets that standard.
